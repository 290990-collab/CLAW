# Guide to marketing a product

For projects that must be found, not only work. Written for someone who does not know the field: what is here is what it takes to decide, not a course.

## Positioning

- **The audience is a person in a situation,** not a category: who has the problem, when they have it, what changed that made them look for a solution, what they use today to work around it. "Developers" is not an audience; "someone reopening their own project after three months who no longer remembers how it works" is.
- **The real alternative is almost always "do nothing":** you compete against inertia before you compete against another product.
- **People switch when the push outweighs what holds them back:** on one side the frustration with the current solution and the pull of the new one, on the other habit and the fear of the switch. The message works on both sides.
- **The promise is a sentence the product keeps today.** In the future tense it is not a promise, it is a debt.
- **One promise only.** Two promises in the same message do not add up, they cancel out: the reader no longer knows what the product is.
- **The same benefit reassures one audience and alarms another:** "new" is an advantage to early adopters and a risk to the cautious. You choose whom you write for, you do not average.
- **A large, frequent problem repays marketing; a small or rare one makes it expensive.** If the product sits on the weak side, say so, because it changes channels, cadence and expectations.

## Research

- **The audience's words are worth more than ours:** how they describe the problem and the solution, verbatim, with source and date. Readers recognise themselves in their own sentences, not in paraphrases.
- **How much a finding holds:** high if it recurs in three or more independent, unprompted sources; medium with two sources, or if the answer was prompted; low with one. It is stated next to the finding.
- **Every source has its bias:** reviews are written by the most expert and the most unhappy, support requests only tell problems, technical forums are more sceptical than the average audience.
- **Try to disprove yourself:** a question that cannot return the unwelcome answer does not inform.
- **Without customers, the profile is a declared hypothesis:** it is drawn from what the product does differently, from the reviews of the alternatives, from nearby products. Every trait carries its source and is replaced by first-hand data as soon as it arrives.

## Evidence

- **Every claim has traceable evidence:** a feature that exists, a measured number, a line of documentation. Without it, it does not go out.
- **A number without a baseline says nothing:** "three times faster" needs compared to what, on what, measured how.
- **The strongest evidence is the least declarative:** an example that runs, a real screenshot, a defect told and fixed.

## Packaging

- **Title and image decide whether the rest is read.** They are written before the content and judged together, never apart.
- **The title states the benefit or the tension, not the category:** "log management tool" describes; "find the line that broke the build" promises.
- **Title test:** preceded by "Now you can…" it must be both desirable and true. A description or a vague promise does not pass.
- **The image is judged at the size it will be seen:** small, on a phone, among others.
- **The destination repeats the title that brought the reader:** whoever arrives and does not find what was promised leaves.

## Channel and cadence

- **The channel decides the form:** length, tone, rhythm and format change from one channel to the next, and the same piece copied everywhere returns less than one adapted.
- **The owned channel is the only one that stays:** site, list, blog. Social media and guest spots lend attention and can take it back, so every release there leads to an owned channel.
- **One main piece, many derivatives:** it is designed to be split from the start, with sections that stand alone and sentences and numbers to lift out, instead of a new piece for every channel.
- **The channel's lifespan sets the rhythm:** where a piece lasts hours frequency counts, where it lasts years depth does.
- **The announcement weighs as much as the change:** a big novelty gets a release across several channels, a small one a line in the release notes. Announcing everything with the same weight uses up the attention that what matters needs.
- **Regularity before volume:** a cadence sustainable for months is worth more than one intense week followed by silence.
- **One piece, one goal:** make known, make try, bring back. A piece chasing all three gets none.

## Measurement

- **The metric is chosen before publishing,** together with the prediction: afterwards, the one that went well gets chosen.
- **Vanity metrics decide nothing:** views and likes measure the packaging; trials, sign-ups and replies measure the promise.
- **A single piece is noise:** the trend is read across several pieces, and a delta inside the usual variability is not a result.
- **A comparison between two versions changes one thing only,** fixes beforehand how many readers it needs and closes there, not when the partial numbers look clear. With a small audience it cannot be measured, and that is said.
- **Disproofs are recorded** like confirmations: not recording them means paying their cost again in two months.

## What is not done

- **No claim the product does not keep,** not even for a better title: the cost is paid once and is not recovered.
- **No invented third-party data:** market numbers, quotes and comparisons carry the source with its date, or they stay out.
- **Nothing nobody has read gets published:** it is irreversible, and the user decides it.
